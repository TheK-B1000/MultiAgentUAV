\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
Each team size plays two scripted opponent regimes, Pole~A and Pole~B, that demand different
team strategies. For strategies $(\pi_A, \pi_B)$ we report the crossover
$\Delta_A = V(\pi_A, A) - V(\pi_B, A)$ and $\Delta_B = V(\pi_B, B) - V(\pi_A, B)$, where $V$ is the
win rate; both are positive when each strategy wins more on its own pole. All cells of a
comparison share the same evaluation seeds and use deterministic actions, so each seed gives a
paired difference in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. We report the mean and standard deviation of that per-seed
difference over $n$ seeds (the standard error is $\mathrm{std}/\sqrt{n}$); 95\% paired bootstrap
intervals are shown in the figures. As a secondary measure we report the same crossover computed
on the final score margin (own score minus opponent score) instead of the win indicator.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Our system}
\label{sec:exp-ours}

Our system pairs a Pole-A strategy with a Pole-B specialist. On Pole~A, a frozen attacker policy
is paired with a learned defender policy under a fixed role allocator: the $k$ agents nearest the
home flag at the start of the episode defend for the whole episode ($k=1$ at $2$v$2$, $k=2$ at
$4$v$4$, $k=1$ at $6$v$6$). The same recipe is used at every team size; only $N$ and $k$ change.

\paragraph{Results.}
Both mean crossovers are positive at every team size (Table~\ref{tab:ours}): each strategy wins
more often on the pole it was built for. The effect is largest on Pole~B at $2$v$2$
($\Delta_B = +0.539$) and $4$v$4$ ($+0.406$) and shrinks sharply at $6$v$6$
($\Delta_A = +0.016$, $\Delta_B = +0.078$). The $6$v$6$ win rates explain part of this: both
strategies win $0.88$--$0.95$ of games on both poles (Table~\ref{tab:ours-winrates}), which leaves
little room for a win-rate difference. On score margin the $6$v$6$ specialists separate clearly
(Table~\ref{tab:ours-margin}): the intended strategy wins by $0.750$ more goals on Pole~A and
$0.625$ more on Pole~B (95\% intervals $[+0.47, +1.02]$ and $[+0.30, +0.95]$), and the $6$v$6$
margin $\Delta_A$ is the largest of the three team sizes. The gap between weak win-rate separation
and clearly positive score-margin separation at $6$v$6$ is consistent with saturation of the
binary win-rate metric at that scale.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:exp-baselines}

\emph{Specialists, no roles} evaluates the two specialists that our system is built from, with no
role allocator: all agents run the Pole-A specialist or all run the Pole-B specialist. The Pole-B
specialist is the same checkpoint in both systems, so the difference isolates the role allocation
on the Pole-A side. The distilled students (Share-Encoder, Fully Shared+$z$) learn from the same
two teachers, on the same teacher states, with the same objective and budget; only what they
share differs. They play $\pi_A$ and $\pi_B$ with their strategy input fixed to $z_0$ and $z_1$.

\paragraph{Results.}
Without the role allocator the specialists do not show the crossover. At $2$v$2$, on matched
seeds, the no-role pair gives $\Delta_A = +0.055$ and $\Delta_B = -0.055$, against $+0.180$ and
$+0.523$ with the allocator (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}). At $4$v$4$ the no-role Pole-A
specialist wins more often than the Pole-B specialist on both poles ($0.813$ vs.\ $0.539$ on
Pole~A, $0.742$ vs.\ $0.586$ on Pole~B; Table~\ref{tab:baselines-winrates}), so $\Delta_B$ is
negative ($-0.156$); with the allocator $\Delta_B$ becomes $+0.406$. Because $\pi_B$ is unchanged,
the change in $\Delta_B$ comes from the Pole-A strategy: once one or two agents are committed to
defence, it wins far less often on Pole~B ($0.648 \to 0.070$ at $2$v$2$, $0.742 \to 0.102$ at
$4$v$4$), i.e., it becomes specific to Pole~A. On Pole~A itself the allocator raises the win rate
at $2$v$2$ ($0.523 \to 0.648$, matched seeds), while at $4$v$4$ the Pole-A strategy with the
allocator wins less often than the no-role specialist ($0.648$ vs.\ $0.813$, different seeds).

The distilled students learn from the no-role specialists, so their reference is the no-role row,
not our system. At $2$v$2$ both keep a positive mean crossover (Share-Encoder $+0.125$/$+0.070$,
Fully Shared+$z$ $+0.078$/$+0.062$; Table~\ref{tab:baselines}), comparable to their teachers'
($+0.055$/$-0.055$, on different seeds): distilling the two strategies into shared parameters does
not by itself remove what the teachers had. Sharing the whole network gives smaller means than
sharing only the encoder. Neither student approaches the Pole-B separation that the role
allocator produces ($\Delta_B \approx +0.5$). Against the Generalist on the same 128 seeds, our
system wins more often on Pole~A ($\Delta_G = +0.195$) and about equally often on Pole~B
($-0.023$; Table~\ref{tab:generalist}): its advantage over a single policy fitted to both teachers
is on Pole~A. Differences in $\Delta_A$ of about $0.1$ between methods should be read with care:
our own $\Delta_A$ is $+0.117$, $+0.180$ and $+0.320$ on three disjoint sets of 128 seeds.

\subsection{Our system under deployment noise}
\label{sec:exp-noise}

We evaluate the final system at each team size under three deployment disturbances at the
medium tier, each against a nominal run on the same seeds. Localization noise
($\sigma = 0.06$ cells) corrupts every position the team observes, including the positions the
role allocator ranks; motion error ($\sigma = 0.06$ cells) perturbs realized motion; control delay
executes each action two decisions (1\,s) late. No training code sees any disturbance.
