\subsection{Specialization Across Team Sizes}

We first evaluate whether the standardized role-allocated system exhibits the
intended strategic specialization across team sizes. For each team size, we
report
\(\Delta_A\), the mean advantage of the Pole-A policy on Pole A, and
\(\Delta_B\), the mean advantage of the Pole-B policy on Pole B. Positive
values indicate specialization in the intended direction.

Following the reporting convention used here, the main text emphasizes the
mean effects, while confidence intervals are reported separately in the
figures. Standard deviations in the tables are computed across the per-seed
paired win differences. Since each per-seed win difference takes values in
\(\{-1,0,+1\}\), the standard deviation can be large relative to the mean.

All three results are confirmatory evaluations with \(n=128\) fresh seeds.
(An earlier exploratory 6v6 evaluation with \(n=64\) seeds gave
\(\Delta_A=+0.016\), \(\Delta_B=+0.078\); it remains on record.)

The mean specialization effects are positive at all three team sizes. The
separation is substantially larger at 2v2 and 4v4 than at 6v6. At 6v6,
$\Delta_A=+0.062$ and $\Delta_B=+0.117$, indicating that specialization
remains in the intended direction in mean, but the difference in binary win
rate between the policies is small.

One possible explanation is saturation of the binary win-rate metric at the
larger team size. Both 6v6 policies achieve high absolute win rates against
the evaluated opponents, leaving relatively little room for specialization to
appear as a difference in final win/loss outcome (on the confirmatory seeds
the two 6v6 policies win between $0.86$ and $0.98$ of games on each pole). We
therefore also examine the paired score margin obtained from the same sealed
128-seed confirmatory evaluation.

Although the 6v6 binary win-rate differences are small, the intended
specialist has a larger score margin on its corresponding pole. The paired
score-margin advantage is \(+0.812\) on Pole A and \(+0.734\) on Pole B, with
both bootstrap intervals remaining above zero (the earlier 64-seed exploratory
evaluation gave \(+0.750\) and \(+0.625\)). This discrepancy between weak
binary win-rate separation and stronger score-margin separation is consistent
with saturation of the binary win-rate metric at 6v6, although it does not by
itself establish that saturation is the cause.


\subsection{Baseline Comparison}

We next compare the proposed role-allocated system against alternative policy
organizations. The specialist baseline executes the repaired Pole-A and
Pole-B policies directly, without the explicit heuristic ATTACK/DEFEND
decomposition. The parameter-sharing baselines use the same standardized
distillation data and evaluation protocol. The Generalist is treated
separately because it consists of a single policy and therefore does not have
a meaningful crossover specialization quantity of the form
\(\Delta_A/\Delta_B\).

Table~\ref{tab:role_baseline_comparison} first isolates the effect of explicit
role allocation. For 2v2, the role-allocation row is the paired diagnostic
evaluated on the same seeds as the corresponding no-role specialist baseline.
It is therefore distinct from the independent 2v2 confirmatory result reported
in Table~\ref{tab:main_specialization}. For 4v4, the available no-role and
role-allocated results come from separate sealed evaluations and should not be
interpreted as a seed-by-seed paired comparison. For 6v6, the no-role baseline
and the confirmatory role-allocated evaluation share the same 128 seeds, so the
comparison is paired.

At 2v2 and 4v4, the repaired specialists without explicit heuristic roles do
not exhibit reciprocal specialization because the Pole-B effect is negative.
Introducing the ATTACK/DEFEND decomposition changes the Pole-B specialization
effect to the intended positive direction while retaining a positive Pole-A
effect.

The paired 2v2 diagnostic shows this most directly:
\(\Delta_B\) changes from \(-0.055\) without heuristic roles to \(+0.523\)
with heuristic roles. In the same diagnostic, the Pole-A policy's win rate on
Pole B decreases from \(0.648\) without the explicit role decomposition to
\(0.070\) after ATTACK/DEFEND roles are introduced. Thus, the role allocation
increases strategic separation primarily by constraining the Pole-A strategy
on the opponent for which it is not intended.

A similar pattern is observed at 4v4. The no-role specialist baseline has
\(\Delta_B=-0.156\), whereas the role-allocated system has
\(\Delta_B=+0.406\). The Pole-A policy's Pole-B win rate decreases from
\(0.742\) without explicit roles to \(0.102\) with the role-allocated system.
Unlike the paired 2v2 diagnostic, however, these 4v4 values come from separate
evaluation seed sets, so the comparison is descriptive rather than a
seed-by-seed paired effect. The 4v4 Pole-A win rate also decreases under the
role-allocated system, from approximately \(0.813\) in the no-role evaluation
to \(0.648\) in the role-allocated evaluation, indicating that the role
decomposition trades some raw Pole-A performance for substantially greater
separation between the two strategic behaviors.

At 6v6, where the comparison is paired on the same 128 seeds, the role
allocation raises $\Delta_B$ from $+0.031$ to $+0.117$, a paired change of
$+0.086$ (95\% interval $[+0.023, +0.148]$), and changes $\Delta_A$ by
$-0.031$ ($[-0.078, +0.016]$). The mechanism is the same as at the smaller
team sizes: with a committed defender, the Pole-A strategy wins less often on
Pole~B ($0.945 \to 0.859$), while the Pole-B specialist is unchanged
($0.977$).

At 2v2, both distilled students retain positive mean specialization, but the
effects are much smaller than for the role-allocated system in
Table~\ref{tab:main_specialization} ($\Delta_A=+0.117$, $\Delta_B=+0.539$).
Share-Encoder reaches $\Delta_A=+0.125$ and $\Delta_B=+0.070$; Fully Shared+$z$
reaches $\Delta_A=+0.078$ and $\Delta_B=+0.062$. Relative to the separated
system, the main loss is on Pole~B: sharing preserves a weak intended-direction
signal, but does not recover the large $\Delta_B$ of the role-allocated
specialists.

At 4v4 the distilled students reproduce their teachers, the no-role specialists
($\Delta_A=+0.273$, $\Delta_B=-0.156$): Share-Encoder reaches $+0.281$ /
$-0.156$ and Fully Shared+$z$ $+0.367$ / $-0.109$. As with the teachers, the
strategy distilled from the Pole-A specialist wins more often on both poles
($0.805$ and $0.680$ for Share-Encoder), so $\Delta_B$ is negative. Sharing
parameters therefore does not remove the specialization the teachers had; at
4v4 the teachers had no Pole-B specialization to preserve, and only the
role-allocated system shows a positive $\Delta_B$.

At 6v6 all policies win most games (0.86--0.98), and the distilled students
show small effects in the intended direction (Share-Encoder $+0.039$ /
$+0.039$, Fully Shared+$z$ $+0.031$ / $+0.008$), close to their no-role
teachers ($+0.094$ / $+0.031$), with the highest held-out agreement with the
teachers of any team size (0.99 / 0.94). As at 2v2 and 4v4, none of the
parameter-sharing baselines reaches the Pole-B effect of the role-allocated
system ($+0.117$).

comparison is reported using absolute win rates on each pole and the
corresponding gap to the proposed role-allocated system rather than a
crossover specialization effect. The gap is formed on matched seeds against a
Separated reference evaluated on the same block.

\subsection{Robustness to Deployment Noise}

Finally, we evaluate the proposed system under deployment-time perturbations.
The robustness experiments consider localization noise, motion error, and
control delay independently at 2v2, 4v4, and 6v6. Training is unchanged; all
perturbations are introduced only during evaluation.

For each perturbation, we report the mean and standard deviation of both
\(\Delta_A\) and \(\Delta_B\). The nominal results in
Table~\ref{tab:main_specialization} provide the corresponding unperturbed
reference. Matched-seed degradation relative to a same-block nominal run will
be reported where available (the 2v2 noise suite uses one matched seed block
for nominal and all three medium-tier disturbances).

The corresponding 2v2 nominal result on the same matched seed block is
\(\Delta_A=+0.219 \pm 0.709\) and
\(\Delta_B=+0.492 \pm 0.517\).
Relative to nominal, the paired mean changes are
\((-0.016,+0.039)\) for localization,
\((+0.016,+0.047)\) for motion, and
\((+0.039,-0.016)\) for delay.
All six 2v2 paired-change 95\% intervals include zero; specialization stays
positive on both poles.

The corresponding 4v4 nominal result is
\(\Delta_A=+0.234 \pm 0.670\) and
\(\Delta_B=+0.484 \pm 0.588\).
Paired changes are
\((-0.016,-0.047)\) (localization),
\((+0.023,-0.117)\) (motion), and
\((-0.094,-0.109)\) (delay).
All six 4v4 change intervals include zero, while mean specialization remains
positive under every disturbance.

The corresponding 6v6 nominal result is
\(\Delta_A=+0.031 \pm 0.434\) and
\(\Delta_B=+0.094 \pm 0.424\).
Localization and motion keep both means positive
(\(+0.039/+0.102\) and \(+0.039/+0.039\)).
Control delay raises \(\Delta_A\) to \(+0.070\) but flips
\(\Delta_B\) to \(-0.047\) (paired change vs nominal about
\((+0.039,-0.141)\)).
Thus specialization is broadly stable under localization/motion, while larger
teams are more sensitive to delayed control on Pole~B.
